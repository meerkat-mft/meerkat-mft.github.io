Meerkat MFT Early development

Managed file transferthat doesn't hold your files.

An orchestrator directs and records every delivery. It can't read your files or credentials, or send them anywhere your agents don't allow.

What is it?

For businesses that can't lose track of a file.

Companies send files to banks, suppliers, and partners all day. Payroll, invoices, patient records, orders.

Managed file transfer makes that automatic, encrypted, and provable. Meerkat is designed so the system in charge never holds the keys to your files.

Why it exists

File transfer servers keep making headlines.

Internet-facing file transfer software has become a favourite target, because one flaw can expose everything passing through it. Meerkat adds no internet-facing server of its own.

The idea

It sees every delivery. Not what's inside.

Meerkat is an open source managed file transfer orchestrator. Agents on your own systems do the reading and writing. The orchestrator is designed to direct and record every delivery while file contents and credentials stay unreadable to it.

Standing orders

Three rules the design doesn't bend.

  1. 01

    Meerkat adds nothing that faces the internet.

    Agents dial out, and the orchestrator and relay belong on a private network. Partners use an endpoint you own, so Meerkat adds no internet-facing server to attack.

  2. 02

    The orchestrator can't read your files or your credentials.

    Files are encrypted between agents, and endpoint credentials are sealed to the agents that use them. Each agent keeps a local allowlist the orchestrator can't change, so it can't send files anywhere else.

  3. 03

    No file moves without evidence.

    Audit records are hash-chained, signed, and checkable outside the database.

How it differs from typical MFT.

The route

Two agents. One blind relay.

Neither agent accepts a connection. Both reach out to the relay, which forwards ciphertext it cannot open.

Every hop is encrypted end to end. Only the two agents hold keys.

Visual flows

Build flows on a canvas.

You build flows by dropping cards onto a canvas and connecting them. Triggers, conditions, destinations, and failure paths stay visible, so an auditor can read one too.

Each delivery leaves a record: name, size, checksum, outcome. Never the contents.

Illustrative example. The canvas is designed, not built.

Your infrastructure

Runs where your data lives.

Meerkat is designed to be self-hosted, on any cloud or in your own data centre. There is no vendor service in the middle, so your files stay in your environment and the region you choose.

Compliance

Records you can show an auditor.

Audit trails, access control, encryption, and retention settings are designed to support your GDPR, NIS2, and DORA work. Those laws bind organizations, not software, so showing compliance stays your job. Meerkat is meant to give you the records to do it.

What's planned

On the drawing board.

  1. Connects to what you have

    Local files first, then SFTP, S3, Azure Blob, and SMB.

  2. API first

    Everything in the console is available through a REST API, for CLIs and ITSM tools.

  3. Recovers from failures

    Interrupted deliveries retry and resume, and source files stay until every delivery succeeds.

  4. How it's built

    Services written in Go, with signed releases, SBOMs, and no custom cryptography.

None of this is built yet.
